How to read it A high return on what you paid is not a high return on your time

The usual buying rule is a multiple: buy it if it sells for three, five, seven times what you paid. It's quick and it protects beginners from overpaying. But a cheap thing still has to be cleaned, photographed, listed, answered about and posted — nearly the same minutes as a dear one. Once your week is full, those minutes are the expensive part.

A worked example

The $2 gadget that sells for $14 is a 7× buy. After a 15% planning rate and $8 of per-item costs it leaves about $1.90 — at 48 minutes, roughly $2.38 an hour. The $14 scale that sells for $48 is only about 3.4×, and leaves about $18.80 — roughly $23.50 an hour. With a $20 hurdle, the most you could pay for the scale is $40.80 − $8 − $16 = $16.80, so at $14 it's a buy; nothing you could pay for the gadget would clear it.

Common questions

Where do my minutes per item come from?

From a one-week time study: every block of reselling time, written down, split across the items it served. An honest average beats a guess — guesses are almost always too low.

What should my hurdle be?

A figure between what your hours earn now and what your best kind of item earns, written down with a date — and raised only on a date, with evidence, never lowered in the aisle.

Is this advice?

No — arithmetic on numbers you enter. Your results depend on your buyers, your costs and the platform's current fees.
